I’m Manish Bhargava, a Computer Science student, software engineer, builder, and someone who has probably learned more by breaking things and trying again than by getting things right the first time.

I’ve always been curious about how things work. But somewhere along the way, that curiosity became more specific: I wanted to build things myself.

Not just write code for the sake of writing code.

I wanted to take an idea, turn it into something real, put it in front of people, watch it break, fix it, improve it, and eventually ship it.

Code is the starting point. Shipping is the point.

That mindset has shaped most of my journey so far.

CHAPTER 01

Where It Started

I started my Computer Science journey at VIT-AP, studying Computer Science and Engineering.

Like most college students, I entered with a vague idea of what I wanted to do and a much clearer idea that I had absolutely no idea what I was doing.

College introduced me to programming properly, but it also introduced me to something more important — the enormous world that exists outside the syllabus.

There were programming problems to solve, projects to build, hackathons to participate in, people doing things I had never seen before, and technologies appearing every few months that made me realize there was always something new to learn.

I started experimenting.

First it was simple development.

Then websites.

Then APIs.

Then databases.

Then larger applications.

And eventually, I became obsessed with understanding what happens behind the interface.

I didn't want to only know how to make a button work.

I wanted to know what happens after someone clicks it.

How does the request reach the server?

How does authentication work?

Where does the data go?

What happens when thousands of people use the application?

What happens when the database becomes slow?

What happens when the API fails?

That curiosity slowly pulled me deeper into software engineering.

CHAPTER 02

I Learned by Building

I've never been particularly good at learning something just because someone told me to learn it.

I understand things much better when I have a reason to build something.

So instead of trying to learn every technology independently, I started building projects that forced me to learn them.

React taught me how frontend applications actually come together.

Backend development taught me about APIs, authentication, databases and business logic.

PostgreSQL and MongoDB taught me that storing data is easy — designing the right data model is a different problem.

Redis introduced me to caching and fast temporary data.

Docker made deployment feel less mysterious.

AWS made me realize that getting something to work locally is only the beginning.

And eventually, these individual pieces started connecting.

I stopped seeing technologies as isolated tools and started seeing them as parts of a system.

CHAPTER 03

The First Time Software Felt Real

One of the biggest changes in my journey happened when I started working on software that wasn't just a college project.

I worked as a Frontend Developer Intern at Bodhi Learning Labs, where I worked with React on production applications and built responsive interfaces and data-heavy dashboards.

One of the projects involved a dashboard with more than 15 React-based charts.

That experience changed something for me.

A college project can work because you know exactly how it works.

A production application is different.

Real users click things in unexpected ways.

Requirements change.

APIs don't always behave perfectly.

UI decisions affect actual users.

And suddenly small engineering decisions matter.

That was one of my first experiences understanding that production software is much more than making something work once.

CHAPTER 04

Then I Went Deeper

Frontend development was where I started, but I didn't want to stay there.

I became increasingly interested in the backend.

I started working with FastAPI, Node.js, Express, PostgreSQL, MongoDB, Redis, REST APIs, authentication, Docker and AWS.

I wanted to understand the complete lifecycle of a request.

From the browser...

to the API...

to the business logic...

to the database...

through caching and background processing...

and finally back to the user.

That eventually led me toward full-stack development.

I started building applications where I was responsible for much more than the UI.

And that's when software started becoming much more interesting.

CHAPTER 05

Building Something People Could Actually Use

One of the projects that taught me a lot was Udhyog Saathi / BillFlow, a B2B SaaS application designed around wholesale and retailer workflows.

The idea was simple on the surface: help businesses manage things like bills, GST invoices, ledgers and ordering.

But building it meant dealing with the messy parts of real software.

Authentication.

Google OAuth.

JWTs.

Database decisions.

APIs.

Deployment.

Server configuration.

Performance testing.

And eventually getting the application running on an actual cloud server.

It wasn't just:

"Here's my GitHub repository."

It was:

"Here's an application. Now make it work."

That distinction changed the way I think about projects.

CHAPTER 06

Then Came Competitive Programming

At some point, I realized that building applications and solving algorithmic problems were developing completely different parts of my brain.

So I started taking Data Structures and Algorithms seriously.

It started with one problem.

Then another.

Then another.

Eventually, it became a habit.

I spent hundreds of hours solving problems on LeetCode, Codeforces and CSES.

I became a LeetCode Knight, crossed 900+ problems, and pushed my rating beyond 1800.

Competitive programming taught me patience.

A problem that looks impossible at first often becomes manageable once you break it into smaller pieces.

It taught me to think about complexity.

It taught me to stop writing the first solution that comes to mind and ask:

Can this be better?

That mindset has stayed with me even outside competitive programming.

CHAPTER 07

Hackathons Changed Me Too

I've always enjoyed environments where you have very little time and somehow need to build something that works.

That naturally pulled me toward hackathons.

I've participated in multiple competitions and worked on projects with teammates under ridiculous deadlines.

One of the biggest milestones was becoming a Smart India Hackathon National Finalist.

There were long nights, incomplete ideas, bugs appearing at the worst possible time, last-minute changes and the usual hackathon chaos.

But that's exactly what I liked about it.

Hackathons taught me that engineering isn't always about having the perfect plan.

Sometimes you have a few hours, limited resources, a vague problem statement and a team looking at you.

You figure it out.

You build.

You test.

You break it.

You fix it.

And somehow, you ship.

CHAPTER 08

My AI Chapter

Then AI became impossible to ignore.

But I wasn't particularly interested in simply asking ChatGPT questions.

I wanted to understand how AI could actually become part of software.

That curiosity took me into Generative AI, RAG, LangChain, LangGraph, tool calling, MCP, vector databases, FastAPI and AI agents.

I started learning how an LLM could interact with tools, retrieve information, reason through workflows and become one component inside a larger application.

That led to my Generative AI internship at Hidden Mind Solutions, where I worked with technologies including LangGraph, tool calling, MCP, FastAPI, RAG, Qdrant, Docker and AWS.

The more I learned, the more I realized that building AI applications isn't simply about choosing a model.

The difficult part is everything around it.

How do you retrieve the right information?

How do you control the workflow?

How do you evaluate the output?

How do you handle failures?

How do you make the system reliable?

How do you connect the model to real tools and real data?

And most importantly:

How do you turn an impressive AI demo into an actual product?

That question is still one of the things that interests me most.

CHAPTER 09

The Project That Pulled Everything Together

One of my strongest projects came from that intersection of software engineering and AI: an AI-powered GitHub Pull Request Reviewer.

The idea was to build a system that could automatically analyze pull requests and provide useful code-review feedback.

But building it wasn't just about calling an LLM.

I had to think about GitHub webhooks, authentication, HMAC verification, asynchronous processing, backend APIs, databases, Redis, AI workflows, deployment and reliability.

I worked with FastAPI, LangGraph, PostgreSQL, Redis, Docker and GitHub APIs, and eventually deployed the system.

The project became more than another GitHub repository.

It was used by 20+ teams.

That was an important moment for me.

Because there's a huge difference between:

"I built this."

and

"Someone actually uses this."

The second one feels different.

CHAPTER 10

I Started Thinking More Like an Engineer

The more projects I built, the less interested I became in simply collecting technologies.

I don't want a portfolio that says:

React.

Node.

Python.

AWS.

Docker.

AI.

Redis.

PostgreSQL.

MongoDB.

Just because I've touched them.

I want to understand why they exist and when they should be used.

Why Redis instead of hitting the database every time?

Why a queue instead of synchronous processing?

Why PostgreSQL instead of MongoDB for a particular problem?

Why a CDN?

Why caching?

Why microservices?

Why not microservices?

Why does latency matter?

Why does throughput matter?

Why does a system fail when everything worked perfectly on my laptop?

Those questions have become much more interesting to me than simply learning another framework.

CHAPTER 11

And Then There Was Dukaan Flow

More recently, I worked on Dukaan Flow, an FMCG distributor ordering platform designed around the realities of retailers, salesmen and order desks.

The problem wasn't particularly glamorous.

Retailers need to place orders.

Salesmen need to manage them.

Office teams need to process them.

Inventory needs to be tracked.

Credit limits need to be respected.

Schemes and free goods need to be calculated.

Orders eventually need to reach existing business systems.

And sometimes the order doesn't even arrive as structured text — it might be a WhatsApp message, a photograph or a Hindi voice note.

That's where the software problem becomes interesting.

Instead of asking:

"How do I build another e-commerce website?"

the question becomes:

"How do I build a system that fits the way this business already works?"

That distinction is something I've started appreciating more and more.

Good software doesn't force people to behave like computers.

It adapts to how people actually work.

CHAPTER 12

I've Also Learned That Software Isn't Everything

Technology is a huge part of my life, but it isn't my entire personality.

I enjoy talking to people.

I like meeting new people, understanding how they think and working with different personalities.

I've learned a lot from teammates, mentors, recruiters, friends and even people I've disagreed with.

I'm also a huge believer in learning through conversations.

Sometimes one random conversation can completely change how you think about a problem.

Outside technology, I enjoy cricket, being outdoors, exploring new places and spending time with people.

I'm also someone who can spend an unreasonable amount of time getting excited about a new idea.

Sometimes that is useful.

Sometimes it means I have ten project ideas open in my head while I'm supposed to be finishing one.

I'm still working on that part.

CHAPTER 13

The Things That Didn't Work

My journey hasn't been a straight line.

There have been projects that didn't work.

Ideas that never got finished.

Applications that broke after deployment.

Interviews that went badly.

Problems I couldn't solve.

Technologies I thought I understood until I actually had to use them.

And plenty of moments where I wondered whether I was moving fast enough.

But I've started realizing that those experiences are probably more valuable than the easy wins.

Every failed project taught me something.

Every difficult interview showed me a gap.

Every production bug forced me to understand something more deeply.

Every rejection made me a little more comfortable with uncertainty.

I'm still learning how to deal with all of that.

CHAPTER 14

Where I Am Now

Today, I'm in the final stretch of my Computer Science degree at VIT-AP, and I'm focused on becoming a stronger software engineer.

I'm particularly interested in roles around:

Software Engineering · Full-Stack Development · Backend Engineering · AI Engineering · Generative AI

The intersection of these areas is where I feel most excited.

I want to build products where the engineering is strong and the AI is actually useful.

Not AI for the sake of saying something has AI in it.

Not another chatbot wrapped around an API.

But software where AI genuinely reduces manual work, improves decision-making, automates repetitive processes or enables something that wasn't practical before.

CHAPTER 15

What's Next?

I don't have every part of my future figured out.

And honestly, I'm okay with that.

Right now, I want to:

  • Become an excellent software engineer.
  • Work on difficult, meaningful engineering problems.
  • Build products that people actually use.
  • Go deeper into backend and distributed systems.
  • Keep learning AI and agentic systems.
  • Improve my system design skills.
  • Keep competing and solving difficult problems.
  • Travel more.
  • Meet more people.
  • Build things with great teams.
  • And eventually create something of my own.

I don't know exactly where that path will take me.

Maybe I'll build a startup.

Maybe I'll spend years becoming a really strong engineer.

Maybe I'll work on AI infrastructure.

Maybe I'll build products that I haven't even imagined yet.

That's part of the fun.

I'm not trying to have everything figured out.

I'm trying to keep getting better.

CHAPTER 16

Still Building

Looking back, the common thread through everything I've done is actually pretty simple.

I get curious.

I start building.

I get stuck.

I learn.

I break something.

I fix it.

I build something bigger.

And then I find something else I don't understand.

So I start again.

That's probably the best description of me right now.

Still learning.
Still building.
Still experimenting.
Still figuring things out.

And hopefully, with every project, every failure, every person I meet and every problem I solve —

becoming a better engineer and a better human along the way.
